## 3. Why these Shorts get 10M+ (layer by layer)
1. **Topic: "you did this today."**
   - Every viewer has done it: eaten, drunk, slept, laughed while eating, swallowed a pill.
   - It adds a mild fear ("am I hurting myself?") and a common belief to settle.
   - The viewer recognises himself, then worries, and has to watch to the end for the answer. They share it with "you do this!".
   - Rare procedures (tumours, braces, toe-to-thumb) sit at 0.02-6M. Everyday bodies win.
2. **Title:**
   - 30 of the 36 Shorts above 10M are a question: *Why … ? / Why shouldn't you … ? / How do … actually … ?*
   - Everyday words come first ("drink ice water", "sleep right after eating") and there is no jargon.
   - The answer is held back until the doctor speaks.
3. **Visual hook (frame 1):**
   - It always opens INSIDE the body: the organ fills the screen, with googly eyes on its top edge (stomach, throat, uterus, vessel, lungs).
   - Often there is a white label box (TRACHEA, INTRAVASCULAR SPACE).
   - There is no human face, no title card and no intro.
4. **Animation hook:**
   - An intruder arrives within 0.3-0.6 s: food drops in, a pill falls, a cut slices open, a clipper comes down.
   - Movement and conflict appear before the first word.
5. **Voice hook:**
   - The first words come at 0.4-1.4 s. They are an exclamation followed by a question, which opens a loop:
     - "Ah! What the heck is this? It burned me." (HM 21M, 0.4 s)
     - "Huh? What's this again?" (17M)
     - "Ow! My head hurts!" (13M)
     - "Whoa, that's a huge cut!" (21M)
   - A second flavour is the careless owner's action: "Open your mouth, honey." / "Alright, time to trim those nails."
6. **Script engine:**
   - Organs act like coworkers stuck with a careless boss (the human = the viewer).
   - "Bro / dude / man" banter, sarcasm, and calling the Brain for backup.
   - The structure: **want → a warning ignored → it backfires → a scary symptom → "Doc, …?" → the condition named → 1-2 lines of mechanism → one simple rule.**
   - Lines are 4-10 words, at 2.7-3.2 words per second.
   - The longest silence is about 1.5 s (3.7 s at most).
7. **Retention:**
   - One locked wide shot per place; the camera does not wander.
   - The motion comes from acting: eyes, mouths, arms, props.
   - The scene changes only when the place changes.
   - Captions show one word at a time, white, in the same spot near the bottom, and each word pops in.
   - Length runs from 23 s (food up the nose, 22M) and 34 s (ice water, 21M) to 60 s. **The shortest Shorts are among the biggest**, because short means more people finish it and it loops.
   - The clinic starts at 58-83% of the runtime (the 21M ice water: 79%).
   - There is no outro, so the last line cuts straight back to the first frame.
8. **Sound:**
   - A light comedy music bed under everything.
   - A cartoon sound effect on every physical event: sizzle, plop, gulp, splash, squish, boing.
   - Loud masters: -8.7 to -10.2 LUFS.
9. **CTA:** none inside the video (none of the 50 scripts asks you to subscribe). The ask is the next Short.
10. **Trust:**
    - The doctor names a real condition ("esophageal spasm", "pill-induced esophagitis"), which gives viewers a new word to share.
    - It ends on one practical rule ("drink a glass of water right after eating").

## 5. The first video: "Why Shouldn't You Drink Ice Water Right After Eating Hot Food?"
Script, shot list and measurements: `videos/011-ice-water-hot-food/script.md`.

**Why this one has the highest odds:**
- Best repeat rate in the niche (3 of 4 uploads at 3M+), inside the second-strongest family.
- A universal habit: hot soup, chai, pizza or noodles, then ice water. It is common everywhere.
- A belief people already argue about ("don't drink cold water after hot food").

**What is copied from the 21M:**
- the title wording;
- the beat order: burn → "get me ice water" → "Fine, I got it" → the flood → it gets worse → "Doc, …?" → the condition named;
- the pain hook at 0.4 s, with almost the same words;
- the length (about 40 s against 34 s);
- the clinic late (about 60%).

**What is upgraded:** the 21M ends on "acute epiglottitis" from "hot-cold shock", which is not established medicine. We keep its scare level but make it true:
- **an esophageal spasm**: very hot or very cold food and drinks are common triggers;
- the food pipe sits right behind the heart, so the pain can feel like a heart attack;
- the advice is Mayo Clinic's: let very hot or cold food and drinks sit for a bit first, and get squeezing chest pain checked at once.

A heart-attack scare is a stronger emotion than swelling. It also invites "this happened to me" comments.

**Honest limits:**
- No video is guaranteed.
- A brand-new channel has no history.
- The proof comes from two channels, and the 21M was uploaded in Aug 2025.
- What we control: proven topic, proven hook timing, proven length and pacing, true facts.

Sources: Mayo Clinic, "Esophageal spasms" (symptoms, causes, diagnosis and treatment); IARC 2016 (very hot drinks above 65 °C are probably carcinogenic).
