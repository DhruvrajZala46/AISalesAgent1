## Number one: the slow smile

7. Number one: the slow smile.
8. Have you ever greeted someone with a smile that switched on instantly, like a light? It can feel a little rehearsed, even when it's sincere.
9. In one study, 100 people rated computer-generated faces whose smiles appeared either very quickly or more gradually.
10. The slower smiles were rated as more genuine, more attractive and more trustworthy.
11. Those were digital faces on a screen, so think of this as a small, useful nudge, not a guarantee.
12. Here's the habit. When you see someone, let your eyes arrive first. Then let the smile build gradually instead of flashing on.
13. It may feel slow to you, but it tends to read as warm and unforced. Try it once in the mirror: see, soften, smile.

## Number two: the comfortable gaze

14. Number two: the comfortable gaze.
15. Have you ever held eye contact for too long and felt it turn awkward? Or looked away too fast and seemed unsure?
16. Researchers showed nearly 500 museum visitors, from 56 countries, video clips of someone looking at them, and asked when it felt too long or too short.
17. The average comfortable length was just over three seconds. Most people landed between two and five seconds, and nobody liked less than one second or more than nine.
18. It reportedly matches some older face-to-face studies, but these were recorded clips shown to museum visitors, so treat three seconds as a rough guide, not a rule. Real comfort varies with the person and the moment.
19. So here's the habit. Meet their eyes and hold the contact for a few comfortable moments, then let your gaze drift away slowly, as if you're thinking, and come back.
20. Follow the other person's comfort, and if they look away, you can too.
21. Don't snap away, and don't reach for your phone. Soft and unhurried tends to read as poised.

## Number three: your phone out of sight

22. Number three: your phone out of sight.
23. Have you ever put your phone on the table "just in case"?
24. In one study, researchers watched 100 pairs of people chatting in cafes.
25. Pairs with a phone visible, on the table or in someone's hand, reported feeling slightly less close and less empathy for each other than pairs without one.
26. The difference was small, and the study can't prove the phone was the cause. It only recorded whether a phone was visible, not whether anyone used it. But it fits with earlier lab work on the same idea.
27. So here's the habit. When you're talking with someone, keep your phone out of sight, in your bag or your pocket, not face down on the table.
28. If you truly need to check it, say so with a smile: "Do you mind if I check this quickly?"
29. Your full attention is one of the most elegant things you can offer.

## Number four: the warm, steady greeting

30. Number four: the warm, steady greeting.
31. Have you ever offered a quick, limp handshake because you felt nervous? We usually think a handshake is just a formality. But it's one of the first things people notice.
32. In a study of 112 people, each person had their hand shaken twice by four trained coders.
33. The researchers found that a firm handshake was linked to being more outgoing and emotionally expressive, and to being less shy and lower in neuroticism, which is a tendency toward worry and emotional ups and downs.
34. Handshake traits were also related to the impressions the coders formed. The researchers suggested that a firm handshake may be an effective form of self-promotion for women.
35. These are links, not proof that a handshake changes how people see you.
36. So here's the habit, and this part is simply good practice, not something the study tested.
37. Offer your hand a little early, palm to palm, with a grip that's firm but never crushing. Add a couple of gentle pumps, your slow smile and a comfortable gaze.
38. And if your greeting is a hug or a cheek kiss, bring the same energy: unhurried, warm and steady.

## Number five: subtle mirroring

42. Number five: subtle mirroring.
43. Have you ever clicked instantly with someone and couldn't say why? Very often, it's because you were quietly matching each other.
44. In a famous experiment, people who talked with someone who subtly mirrored their posture and mannerisms ended up liking that person more, and the conversation felt smoother. The people being mirrored never noticed.
45. Now, this doesn't mean copying anyone. So here's the habit. After a few moments of conversation, gently match their pace and energy.
46. If they speak softly and slowly, soften your voice and slow down. If they lean in, you can lean in a little too.
47. Match the feeling, not the exact movement. If it ever feels obvious, you're doing too much. The goal is attunement, not imitation.

## Number six: the graceful recovery

48. Number six: the graceful recovery.
49. Have you ever spilled something, dropped your keys or stumbled over your words, and felt the whole room stop? A classic experiment looked at exactly this.
50. In a well-known 1966 experiment, people listened to recordings of two quiz contestants. One was very capable, and one was average. In some versions of the recording, the contestant spilled coffee.
51. Afterwards, the capable contestant who spilled was rated as more likable than the one who didn't, while the average contestant who spilled was rated as less likable.
52. That doesn't mean mistakes always help you. It was an old study, with audio recordings and male students, the blunder was one small spill, and researchers still debate why it happened.
53. So I'm not promising that a slip makes anyone more likable. The part that is simply good sense is this: how calmly you handle a slip shapes how it feels in the room.
54. So here's the habit. When something goes wrong, pause. Take a slow breath out.
55. Give a small smile, say something simple like "Oops, let me fix that," and carry on at the same pace.
56. No spiral of apologies. Staying calm and moving on keeps the moment small.

## Number seven: you look calmer than you feel

57. Number seven: you look calmer than you feel.
58. Have you ever felt your heart racing in a room and been sure everyone could see it?
59. Researchers call this the illusion of transparency. In one public speaking study, speakers rated their own anxiety more harshly than the people watching them did.
60. And the speakers who were simply told about this effect felt less stressed and were rated better by observers. In other words, our nerves are far less visible than we think.
61. So here's the habit. Before you walk in, silently tell yourself: "I look calmer than I feel." Then take one slow breath out and let your shoulders drop.
62. It sounds almost too simple, but the less you try to hide your nerves, the less they show.
