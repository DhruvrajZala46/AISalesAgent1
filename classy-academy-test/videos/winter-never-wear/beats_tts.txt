Number one, a chunky sweater squeezed under a fitted coat.
We've all done this. It's freezing, so you grab your thickest knit, pull your coat over the top, and suddenly you can barely bend your arms. It feels like the warmest choice.
But here's what most people don't realize. What keeps you warm isn't really the fabric itself. It's the still air trapped inside it.
When a thick sweater is pressed tight against the inside of a coat, both get squashed, and that warm pocket of air gets squeezed out.
So you look bulkier, your coat pulls across the shoulders and buttons, and you're often not as warm as you'd expect.
Instead, try a fine merino or cashmere knit under your coat. It's thin, but it holds warmth beautifully without the bulk.
And if you love your chunky cable knit, and honestly, who doesn't, let it be the star. Wear it on its own on milder days or under a coat cut roomy enough to fit over it.
When you shop for a coat, try it on over the thickest sweater you plan to wear. If you can lift your arms and button it without pulling, that's the one. So chic.
Number two, cotton next to your skin on busy, rushing around days.
Cotton is lovely. Soft, breathable, easy. But in winter, there's one situation where it quietly works against you.
Cotton soaks up moisture and dries slowly, and once it's damp, it loses much of its ability to keep you warm. And that moisture doesn't only come from the rain.
Think about a typical winter day. You hurry to the train, walk briskly in your coat, step into a heated shop or office, and you start to warm up.
Your cotton tee absorbs that little bit of sweat and holds on to it. Then you step back outside, and suddenly you feel colder than when you left the house. Sound familiar?
Instead, choose a base layer that handles moisture better. Merino wool is a beautiful option.
Wool can absorb around a third of its weight in moisture vapor before it even starts to feel wet, so it stays comfortable as you move between the cold street and a warm room.
A thin silk layer or a good synthetic thermal works well, too, and dries quickly.
And here's a little trick hikers swear by. Dress for how you'll feel ten minutes into your walk, not the moment you open the door.
Now, this doesn't mean cotton is off limits. On a dry day when you're mostly indoors, your favorite cotton shirt is perfectly fine. It's the damp, active, in-and-out days where it lets you down.
Number three, the cozy acrylic sweater.
It looks soft on the hanger, it's often a fraction of the price, and it feels warm the moment you put it on.
But acrylic absorbs very little moisture. So when you go from the cold outside into a heated room, the warmth and moisture your body gives off has nowhere to go. That's why some acrylic knits feel toasty one minute and strangely clammy the next.
And many acrylic sweaters pill quickly. Those little fuzzy balls that appear under the arms and along the sides after just a few wears.
And as we've said before on this channel, nothing takes away from an elegant look faster than clothing that looks worn down.
Instead, flip to the label before you buy. Look for wool, merino, cashmere, or a wool-rich blend.
Then give the knit a gentle stretch. A quality knit feels dense and springs right back into shape. If it stays stretched out or you can see straight through it, put it back.
Now, a little acrylic in a blend isn't a bad thing. It can make a sweater more affordable and easier to care for. Just let it be the supporting fiber, not the whole sweater.
And washing your knits inside out on a gentle cycle helps them stay smooth for so much longer.
And here's a secret many well-dressed women don't share. They know exactly what flatters them. The colors that make their skin glow in that soft winter light and the coat shapes that complement their figure.

Number four, boots that fit perfectly in October.
You buy them in early fall, wear them with thin socks, and they fit like a dream. Then winter arrives, you add your thickest socks, and suddenly they're snug across the toes.
It feels like you've just made them warmer, but often it's the opposite. When your boots are too tight, they squeeze your feet, and that can restrict the blood flow that keeps your toes warm.
The tightness also crushes the insulation in your socks and in the boot itself. So the thickest sock in a tight boot can leave you with colder feet than a thinner sock with a little room.
Instead, aim for enough space to wiggle your toes freely while your heel stays snugly in place. A fine merino sock gives you warmth without the bulk.
And when you shop for winter boots, try them on in the afternoon, wearing the socks you'll actually wear with them.
Of course, you don't want boots that are too loose either. If your foot slides around, cold air moves around inside with it. The goal is room for your toes and support for your heel.
Number five, smooth soles on icy days.
There's nothing more elegant than a sleek leather boot or a pointed-toe heel with a long coat. But on ice, a smooth sole has almost nothing to grip with. And no outfit looks graceful while you're tiptoeing down the pavement or worse, slipping.
Here's something that surprised me. Researchers at Toronto's Winter Lab tested nearly a hundred types of winter footwear on ice, and fewer than one in ten met their minimum standard for grip.
The ones that performed best had special soles with tiny grit-like particles built right into the rubber. So even a boot sold as a winter boot isn't automatically safe on ice.
Instead, keep one pair with a deeply treaded rubber sole for icy mornings. Many now come in sleek black or brown leather that look just as polished.
You can also ask a cobbler to add a thin rubber sole protector to your favorite leather-soled shoes. It's affordable and adds grip.
And for truly icy days, a pair of slip-on ice grips can live in your bag and come off the moment you arrive.
Now, on dry, cleared pavement, wear your elegant soles and enjoy them. It's simply about knowing which days call for something more.
Number six, thin, unlined leather gloves.
Leather gloves are a winter classic and for good reason. They're timeless, sleek, and they finish a coat beautifully.
But a very thin, unlined pair on a freezing, windy day leaves just a fine layer of leather between your fingers and the cold.
And when your hands are cold, you tense up. Your shoulders rise, you rush, you hide your hands in your sleeves. Not exactly the calm, composed presence we're going for.
Instead, look for leather gloves lined in cashmere, wool, or fleece. They keep the same slim, elegant shape with far more warmth inside.
And on the coldest days, consider mittens. When they're made from the same materials, mittens are warmer than gloves because your fingers share their warmth instead of each one facing the cold on its own.
A soft lined leather or knit mitten looks incredibly chic with a long wool coat. And if you need your fingers free, a thin liner glove underneath a mitten gives you the best of both.
Number seven, nothing on your head because you don't want to ruin your hair.
I completely understand this one. You've done a beautiful blowout, and the last thing you want is hat-hair.
And you may have heard that we lose most of our body heat through our heads. That's actually a myth.
Researchers writing in the British Medical Journal traced it back to a flawed interpretation of an old military experiment. Your head loses heat much like any other uncovered part of your body.
But here's what does matter. Your ears are one of the areas most vulnerable to frostbite, along with your fingers, toes, nose, cheeks, and chin. And on a windy day, cold, stinging ears can make you miserable in minutes.
So instead of skipping it altogether, choose something that protects your ears and your hairstyle.
A wide knit or cashmere headband keeps your ears covered and looks beautiful with a sleek low bun or a soft blowout.
A wool beret is timeless and effortlessly chic. And if you love a beanie, choose a fine merino or cashmere one rather than a stiff, heavy knit. Just make sure your hair is completely dry before you put it on. So pretty.
Saving one of my favorites for last. Number eight, the coat that only looks warm.
Your coat is often the first thing people notice in winter, and it's the piece doing most of the work to keep you warm.
But two coats can look almost identical on the hanger and feel completely different on a windy street. The difference is often the fabric.
A loosely woven coat can let the wind cut straight through it, no matter how thick it looks. Dense, tightly woven, or felted wool fabrics like melton or boiled wool hold up far better against the wind, and they keep their shape beautifully year after year.
Before you buy, check the label. A high wool content is what you're looking for. And a small amount of nylon or polyester is perfectly fine. It can actually make a coat more durable.
Then try this simple test. Hold the fabric up to a window or a bright light. If light shines easily through the weave, chances are the wind will, too.
And finally, look at the length. On truly cold, windy days, a coat that covers your hips and upper thighs protects far more of you than a cropped style. And that long, clean line always looks elegant.
When you find that coat, the right weave, the right weight, the right fit, it becomes the piece you reach for every single winter. That's quiet luxury in its truest form.
